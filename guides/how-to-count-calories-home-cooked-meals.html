How to Accurately Count Calories in Home-Cooked Meals & Batch Prep

By CalorieCalc Nutrition Team • 10 min read • Updated September 2026

Cooking at home is undoubtedly the healthiest way to eat in a calorie deficit. However, many dieters struggle to calculate nutritional values when cooking multi-ingredient dishes like stews, pasta bakes, or stir-fries.

Trying to guess what a ladle of chili contains is inaccurate and stressful. Fortunately, calculating the exact calories and macros per serving of any homemade meal requires just one basic formula and a kitchen scale.

Core Rules for Homemade Food Logging
  • Always Weigh Raw Ingredients: Raw weight is the gold standard because moisture loss during cooking varies drastically.
  • Weigh the Final Finished Pot: The finished dish weight divided by the total recipe calories gives you your exact calorie-per-gram ratio.
  • Save Your Custom Recipes: Creating a recipe once in your tracker allows you to log a bowl in seconds anytime you cook it.

The 3-Step Recipe Math Formula

Step 1: Total Recipe Calories = Sum of all raw ingredient calories
Step 2: Calorie Per Gram = Total Recipe Calories ÷ Total Weight of Cooked Dish in Grams
Step 3: Your Portion Calories = Portion Weight in Grams × Calorie Per Gram

Practical Example: You cook a beef stew with 2,400 total raw calories. The completed stew in the pot weighs 1,200 grams. Each gram contains 2 calories (2,400 ÷ 1,200). If you ladle a 300g bowl for lunch, your meal is exactly 600 calories.

Raw vs. Cooked Weights: Why Moisture Misleads

A 100g raw chicken breast yields about 75g after cooking because water evaporates. Conversely, 100g of dry pasta or raw rice absorbs water and weighs about 225–250g cooked. If you log 200g of cooked pasta using the "dry pasta" entry in your tracker, you will record nearly triple the actual calories! Always ensure the database item matches your weighed state (dry/raw vs. cooked/boiled).

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to weigh non-starchy vegetables like lettuce or cucumbers?

Leafy greens and watery vegetables (cucumbers, celery) have very low caloric density (10–15 calories per cup). While precision is good, estimating greens will rarely affect your weight loss trajectory.

What if someone else in the family cooked the meal?

Ask for the primary protein source and cooking fat used. Log the estimated meat portion and add 1–2 tablespoons of oil to account for butter or cooking oils used in the pan.
